Section 01 — The Threats

Six Ways We're
Poisoning the Sea

Human activity has unleashed multiple simultaneous crises on the world's oceans. Each alone would be catastrophic. Together, they're an extinction-level event in slow motion.

🧴

Plastic Pollution

Single-use plastics, fishing gear, and microplastics now permeate every ocean layer — from surface waters to the deepest trenches. Plastic fragments have been found in polar ice, in fish stomachs, and in human blood.

380M tonnes of plastic produced per year globally
🌡️

Ocean Warming

The ocean absorbs over 90% of the excess heat trapped by greenhouse gases. Warmer water holds less oxygen, disrupts breeding cycles, bleaches coral, and drives fish populations toward the poles — away from communities that depend on them.

2x speed of ocean warming has doubled since 1993
🏭

Chemical & Nutrient Runoff

Agricultural fertilizers, pesticides, sewage, and industrial discharge pour into coastal waters. Excess nitrogen and phosphorus trigger algal blooms that consume all oxygen, creating vast "dead zones" where nothing can survive.

700+ dead zones identified worldwide — up from 45 in the 1960s
🐟

Overfishing

Industrial fleets equipped with sonar and mile-long nets can locate and capture entire schools in a single pass. Roughly 35% of global fish stocks are fished beyond sustainable limits. Bycatch — unintended species caught and discarded — kills millions of dolphins, turtles, and seabirds annually.

35% of fish stocks are overfished — up from 10% in 1974
🔊

Noise Pollution

Shipping traffic, military sonar, seismic surveys, and offshore drilling have turned the ocean into a cacophony. Whales, dolphins, and fish rely on sound for navigation, hunting, and communication. Chronic noise causes stress, disorientation, strandings, and reproductive failure.

100x increase in ocean noise levels since the 1950s
⚗️

Ocean Acidification

The ocean absorbs roughly a quarter of all CO₂ emitted by humans, causing seawater to acidify at a rate faster than any point in the last 300 million years. Acidic water dissolves the shells and skeletons of corals, oysters, mussels, and plankton — the base of the marine food web.

26% more acidic than pre-industrial levels

Section 02 — The Consequences

Who Pays the Price

The ocean's crisis is not distant or abstract. It cascades directly through ecosystems into communities, bodies, and futures around the world.

Effects on Marine Animals

Entanglement & Ingestion

Over 700 species are affected by marine plastic. Sea turtles mistake plastic bags for jellyfish. Albatrosses feed bottle caps to chicks. Sperm whales are found beached with stomachs full of plastic bags and fishing line.

Coral Reef Collapse

Coral reefs — called the "rainforests of the sea" — support 25% of all marine species despite covering less than 1% of the ocean floor. Warming and acidification cause mass bleaching events that kill entire reef systems. The Great Barrier Reef has bleached six times since 1998.

Toxin Accumulation (Bioaccumulation)

Heavy metals, PCBs, and pesticides accumulate up the food chain. Small fish absorb toxins, are eaten by larger fish, which are eaten by larger predators. Top predators like orca whales carry some of the highest toxic loads on the planet.

Habitat Destruction

Bottom trawling — dragging heavy nets across the seafloor — destroys ancient coral gardens, sponge reefs, and seagrass beds that took centuries to form in a single pass. An estimated 4.9 million km² of seafloor is trawled annually.

Effects on Humans

Microplastics in Human Bodies

Microplastics have been found in human blood, breast milk, placentas, and lungs. Studies link microplastic exposure to inflammation, disruption of hormones, and potential links to cancer. The average person consumes a credit card's worth of plastic per week through food and water.

Food Security Crises

3 billion people depend on fish as their primary protein source. Overfishing, pollution, and warming are collapsing fish populations. Coastal communities in West Africa, the Pacific Islands, and Southeast Asia are already experiencing food insecurity as catches decline by up to 30%.

Toxic Shellfish & Algal Blooms

Nutrient runoff fuels harmful algal blooms (HABs) that produce toxins poisonous to fish, shellfish, marine mammals, and humans. HAB events have paralyzed the shellfish industries of entire coastlines and sickened thousands with neurological symptoms.

Climate Destabilization

A healthy ocean regulates global temperature and generates half of the world's oxygen via phytoplankton. As ocean health deteriorates, its ability to absorb CO₂ and produce oxygen weakens — accelerating the very climate change destroying it. It is a feedback loop with no natural brake.

"The ocean is a central nervous system for this planet. When it suffers, nothing — nothing — is left untouched." — Sylvia Earle, Marine Biologist & National Geographic Explorer-in-Residence
Section 03 — A History of Harm

How We Got Here

The ocean crisis didn't happen overnight. It's the cumulative result of a century of industrial growth, political inaction, and willful ignorance.

1950s
Industrial Fishing Expands Globally
Post-WWII technology — sonar, refrigeration, diesel engines — enables industrial fishing fleets. Global fish catch quadruples in 30 years. Signs of stock depletion emerge within a decade but go unaddressed.
1960s
First Dead Zones Identified
Scientists document the first coastal hypoxic zones — areas drained of oxygen by agricultural runoff. The Chesapeake Bay, the Baltic Sea, and the Gulf of Mexico show alarming oxygen depletion. Only 45 dead zones exist. Today there are over 700.
1969
Santa Barbara Oil Spill Shocks the World
A Union Oil blowout spills 3 million gallons of crude oil along 35 miles of California coast. Images of oil-soaked birds galvanize the modern environmental movement and lead to the creation of Earth Day and the EPA.
1980s
Great Pacific Garbage Patch Forms
Ocean currents concentrate plastic debris in the North Pacific subtropical gyre. What begins as a disturbing observation becomes a floating debris field now estimated at 1.6 million km² — twice the size of Texas — with a density of 80,000 tonnes of plastic.
1998
First Global Coral Bleaching Event
An El Niño event combined with rising baseline temperatures triggers the first global mass coral bleaching, killing 16% of the world's coral reefs in one year. Scientists warn it is a preview of the future under continued warming.
2019–2020
Great Barrier Reef's Worst Bleaching on Record
Back-to-back bleaching events, the worst ever recorded, affect over 50% of the Great Barrier Reef's corals. Scientists describe it as the reef entering a new, permanently degraded state. Surveys reveal coral recovery from previous bleaching events has stalled.
2023
Ocean Surface Temperature Hits Record High
The average global ocean surface temperature reaches an all-time high, shattering previous records by an unprecedented margin. Marine heatwaves — devastating to marine life — are now occurring five times more frequently than in the 1980s.
Section 04 — What Can Be Done

It Is Not Too Late.
But the Window Is Closing.

Ocean ecosystems are resilient when given the chance. Marine protected areas, reduced emissions, and clean technology have already shown measurable recovery where implemented. The science is clear. What's needed is will.

01

Expand Marine Protected Areas

Less than 3% of the ocean is fully protected. Closing critical spawning grounds, migration corridors, and reef systems to extraction allows ecosystems to recover. Studies show fish populations inside MPAs increase 4-fold within a decade — and spill over into surrounding waters.

02

End Single-Use Plastic at Scale

Over 80% of ocean plastic originates from just 20 rivers. Upstream intervention — banning single-use plastics, improving waste infrastructure in high-impact regions, and holding producers financially responsible — can dramatically reduce the flow before it reaches the sea.

03

Decarbonize Energy Systems

Stabilizing ocean temperature and acidity requires cutting CO₂ emissions. Renewable energy transitions directly protect the ocean. Every fraction of a degree of warming prevented is a future coral reef that doesn't bleach, an ice sheet that doesn't melt, a fishery that doesn't collapse.

04

Reform Industrial Fishing

Ending harmful fishing subsidies ($22 billion per year globally), eliminating destructive trawling practices, and enforcing science-based catch limits would allow fish populations to rebuild. Sustainable aquaculture, when well-managed, can meet global protein needs without depleting wild stocks.

05

Invest in Ocean Science

We know less about the deep ocean than the surface of Mars. Increased funding for ocean monitoring, deep-sea research, and ecosystem modeling gives policymakers the data needed to make evidence-based decisions before critical tipping points are crossed.

06

Support Coastal Communities

Billions of people live at the intersection of ocean health and human survival. Supporting sustainable local fisheries, mangrove restoration (which protects 18% of the world's coastlines), and climate-resilient livelihoods builds the human infrastructure for long-term ocean stewardship.
